Anmeldung (Registering Address) in Berlin

Anmeldung

2026-10-19 About 15 mins
#Berlin#Anmeldung#Registering#Address

Back to Berlin

Since there are no in-person classes during my final semester, I’ve moved back to Berlin. While Aachen is a small, beautiful, and practical city, Berlin just feels a bit more like home to me.

Just as you would register your residence in Korea, any change of address in Germany requires notifying the local authority. It is typically the most straightforward and initial administrative procedure you encounter here, yet if you aren’t fluent in German, it can feel like a massive wall. My German is still extremely rusty, but somehow, I’ve managed to get through all three of my Anmeldungen so far.

An-/Um-/Abmeldung

Anmeldung is similar to Korea’s address registration (전입신고) and applies when you move to Germany for the first time or relocate to a residence outside your previous municipal jurisdiction. You are required to register within 14 days. Ummeldung applies when you move to a new address within the same jurisdiction. Abmeldung applies when your new residence is located outside Germany—meaning you are leaving the country altogether. 1

Since I am registering a change of address from Aachen to Berlin, it falls under Anmeldung, not Ummeldung or Abmeldung. Back when I did my Anmeldung in Aachen, I was told that my records at the local citizen’s office (Bürgeramt) would automatically transfer from Berlin to Aachen. I assumed the same applied to the Immigration Office (Ausländeramt, Landesamt für Einwanderung, or Migrationsamt) and the Tax Office (Finanzamt). However, the Aachen Immigration Office required a one-time in-person visit to transfer and process my file, while the Berlin Tax Office demanded justification regarding my primary/secondary residence. While you certainly don’t need to file a separate departure notice with your previous local office, it definitely pays to check how the specific administrative authorities at both ends handle their procedures.

Documents

There are two required documents:

  1. Registration Form (Anmeldung bei der Meldebehörde)
  2. Landlord Confirmation Form (Wohnungsgeberbestätigung)

Registration Form

Use the Registration Form Template provided by the ServicePortal Berlin.

Landlord Confirmation Form

If your landlord or real estate agency provided a document when you signed the lease, you can use that directly. When I registered in Aachen previously, I submitted the document prepared by the agency. If not, you can request them to fill out the Landlord Confirmation Sample Form provided by the ServicePortal Berlin.

Booking a Termin

If your move-in date is fixed, booking a Termin (appointment) actually takes priority over the move itself or preparing documents. The first time I registered in Berlin, and again in Aachen, getting an appointment for this simple administrative task meant waiting anywhere from 3 weeks to two full months. If you’re already late, your best bet is refreshing the booking page whenever you think of it, or hunting early in the morning when new slots or cancellations are released. Or… you can just snag whatever appointment is left anywhere in the city and treat yourself to a little tour of the Berlin U-Bahn.

In stark contrast to my past struggles, I logged onto the booking page this time with zero expectations, only to secure an appointment effortlessly. I even got to pick the Bürgeramt closest to my apartment and my preferred time slot. This is unheard of in Berlin, leaving both me and the locals I told completely stunned.

Online Termin

You can select a nearby Bürgeramt and choose an available date and time slot from the List of Local Offices on the Wohnsitz page on the ServicePortal Berlin.

No Online Anmeldung

Despite having an eID-enabled card and a BundID account, online Anmeldung wasn’t an option. Non-EU citizens are strictly required to book an appointment and process the registration on-site in writing. Online Anmeldung is reserved exclusively for German and EU citizens.2

The Appointment

First Attempt (2026-10-02)

I walked into the Bürgeramt with full confidence, only to run headfirst into a bizarre situation. When I registered in Aachen, my Berlin address had been registered as a secondary residence (Nebenwohnung). This was precisely why the Berlin Tax Office had previously demanded an explanation. Since Berlin is one of the cities that taxes secondary residences, you must avoid being registered with a Nebenwohnung when relocating to another city if you no longer live there or maintain the address.

Since I had brought my binder full of administrative records and documents (expats jokingly say the very first thing you must do upon arriving in Germany is buy a binder to collect paperwork), I quickly flipped through my Aachen Anmeldung files. Fortunately, my original application form was right there, where I had correctly listed my new Aachen address as my sole (einzige) residence and marked my Berlin address as deregistered (abgemeldet). Lacking the confidence to explain the situation in German, I asked if I could briefly explain my situation in English, only to receive a prompt, blunt “No.”

Eventually, an English-speaking employee nearby stepped in to help, took a look at the system, and broke the news:

“You have registered your address in Berlin as Nebenwohnung on [date] November last year.”

“Well.. no. That was not my intention. Could you please have a look at my applica…[tion form here]?”

Cutting me off mid-sentence, the reply came: “There is no ‘No’. That’s what we have on the system.”

To be fair, the civil servants I’ve encountered so far have been friendly enough (by German standards). I’m even grateful they don’t kick you out for not bringing a German translator like they used to. However, when a problem arises, I’ve noticed a recurring pattern: instead of offering potential solutions as experts who know the administrative process (not that I expect them to solve it for me…), they resort to a completely defensive posture.

And just like that, I walked out with nothing solved and the absurd response that there was nothing they could do in Berlin, suggesting I go all the way to the Aachen Bürgeramt in person to talk it out.

HW/NW

There is a difference between the document requiring your signature (retained by the authority) and the confirmation document issued to you after processing. I don’t recall the exact layout of the Aachen form, but the issued document contained no information about a secondary residence at all—despite the official’s misprocessing that kept my Berlin address as a Nebenwohnung! (Presumably, addresses outside their jurisdiction simply aren’t displayed.) It listed only “Hauptwohnung” (Since there was no annotation column like in Berlin, it should have been einzige or alleinige. Back then, I didn’t quite grasp the semantic nuance between einzige and Hauptwohnung.) On the form requiring a signature in Berlin, previous and future address statuses—whether sole, primary, or secondary, and whether maintained or deregistered—are explicitly marked for both locations. Conversely, the document I received simply stated “HW,” with a footnote reading “HW = Alleinige oder Hauptwohnung” (HW = Sole or Primary Residence).

Long story short, since things can be processed entirely contrary to your intentions depending on the official’s “Kibun” (mood/caprice), it’s best to keep a copy of your completed application or store it carefully if they return it to you (which is also completely random…).

Data Processing (2026-10-12)

Fortunately, the city of Aachen helped me resolve the error via email very quickly without requiring a personal visit. If you ever run into similar trouble, as the data subject, you can cite Sections 63 and 124 of the German Federal Act on Registration (Bundesmeldegesetz) regarding your right to data self-determination, accuracy, and completeness.

I booked another Termin (slots were surprisingly still plentiful!) and visited the Bürgeramt after the weekend, only to be told that the system hadn’t updated yet and to come back after waiting another week.

Done (2026-10-19)

For the first time, no appointments were available at my local neighborhood Bürgeramt, so I grabbed a single remaining or canceled slot at the adjacent office and visited. A civil servant with a thoroughly businesslike expression finally handed over the stamped Anmeldung document. It took about three weeks in total.

Survey

When booking a Termin, the city of Berlin asks whether you’d like to participate in a follow-up survey. I used to opt out every time out of laziness, but a German friend once told me a story that they only ever encountered friendly officials on the rare occasions they opted in…
